Pseudostatic seismic design of slopes requires a horizontal coefficient representing earthquake loading on the sliding mass. In this report the coefficient is derived from displacement-based performance: the Newmark permanent displacement $D_n$ is the slope-deformation performance measure, and the coefficient $k_{\max}$ is the lowest yield coefficient $k_y$ consistent with a performance objective defined by the allowable displacement $D_a$ and the target maximum probability $p_e$ that $D_n$ exceeds $D_a$. The normalized coefficient $k_h$ expresses $k_{\max}$ as a percentage of the site-condition PGA after site response is accounted for in each geometry--material combination, and the coefficients are reported by geometry, material and seismic demand. For the slopes considered, $D_n$ is estimated probabilistically by combining empirical regression models within a lognormal framework.

The horizontal pseudostatic coefficient is derived in this assessment from the allowable permanent displacement $D_a$ of the potentially sliding mass [@BrayTravasarou2009; @MacedoEtAl2018]. The seismic demand and the fundamental periods established in the preceding chapters allow the $D_n (k_y)$ relations between the Newmark permanent displacement and the yield coefficient to be computed; their inversion yields $k_{\max}$, and its normalization by the PGA transformed to the $V_{S30}$ of each geometry--material combination yields $k_h$, so that the result traceably links the deformation objective to the horizontal action employed in the stability verification. The yield acceleration $a_y$ represents the acceleration level that mobilizes the available resistance along the potential failure mechanism, and its dimensionless form is $k_y=a_y/g$; for each allowable displacement and target exceedance probability, the analysis determines the $k_y$ threshold that satisfies the performance criterion, and that threshold is $k_{\max}$, the coefficient applied in the pseudostatic limit-equilibrium verification.

In the Newmark sliding-block method [@Newmark1965], the potentially sliding wedge is represented as a rigid block resting on an inclined plane: relative motion begins when the driving acceleration exceeds the yield acceleration $a_y=k_y g$ and continues until the relative velocity returns to zero, and the permanent displacement is the sum of those episodes over the duration of the earthquake motion, obtained by double integration of the acceleration excess. The extension of the procedure to dams and embankments [@MakdisiSeed1978] derived the equivalent acceleration of the sliding mass from its dynamic response and is the direct antecedent of the flexible formulations. The adopted empirical relations represent the accumulated displacement statistically through two idealizations: rigid-block models relate the displacement to measures of the base excitation, and flexible-block models additionally incorporate the dynamic response of the mass through $T_s$ and the spectral acceleration evaluated at a period proportional to $T_s$.

The displacement calculation uses the seismic demand and the fundamental periods established in Chapter [-@sec-slope_periods]. For each geometry, material and seismic demand -- the probabilistic spectrum of each evaluated return period and the MCE scenario -- a joint sample of the intensity measures required by the rigid-block and flexible formulations is constructed; that sample is common to all models, so their differences represent the displacement formulation rather than incompatible seismic demands. Two inputs feed that sample. The probabilistic input for each return period is the reference-rock UHS, which for 10,000 years is the MDE and is represented at each period by a lognormal distribution whose mean is the UHS ordinate, and the MCE input is the reference-rock MCE spectrum of the site, given by its mean and its quantiles 0.05 to 0.95; both are defined for $V_{S30}=V_{\mathrm{ref}}=760$ m/s. For each geometry and material scenario, the spectral ordinates and the PGA are transformed to the corresponding $V_{S30}$ through the `ST20` amplification model [@Stewart2020; @Hashash2020].

At the site condition of each scenario, the joint sample comprises PGA, $S_a (1.3 T_s)$, and $S_a (1.5 T_s)$. The three measures are represented through their marginals in $\ln S_a$, the site lognormal mixture of each return period, truncated at the truncation level of the hazard calculation, or a spline through the quantiles of the MCE site spectrum, and through a Gaussian copula with the full inter-period correlation matrix [@BakerJayaram2008], with the correlation of the PGA evaluated at the period of 0.01 s; the samples are recentered to preserve the mean of each amplified marginal; in each realization, the three intensities remain paired, and each displacement formulation uses the measures entering its regression. The Arias intensity used by `JB07` and `SR08` completes the set of intensity measures; it is estimated for each realization from the PGA [@Verri2023a], and the relation is applied deterministically, so that each PGA realization determines a single value of $I_A$ and the displacement sample propagates the PGA variability.

For each scenario, 30 values of $k_y$ are evaluated, logarithmically spaced between 0.01 and $\max (\overline{\mathrm{PGA}},0.80)$, where $\overline{\mathrm{PGA}}$ is the mean site-condition PGA of the scenario. That interval establishes the computed range of the $D_n (k_y)$ relations, over which every formulation is evaluated, including the values of $k_y$ outside the range of the data of its source, and over which the inversion to $k_{\max}$ operates.
